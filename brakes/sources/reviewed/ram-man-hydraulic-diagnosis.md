## The Ram Man companion material

The Ram Man's hydraulic information sheet describes two-person bleeding as a diagnostic observation, not merely a fluid-changing operation: one person applies the pedal while another watches fluid flow at the bleeders. Their stated point is that poor/no flow can expose restrictions or component/circuit problems. The same sheet treats bench bleeding as an opportunity to observe whether both master-cylinder outlets flow and whether the cylinder leaks at the rear.

A separate Ram Man dual-hydraulic-system service document emphasizes that a tandem master cylinder preserves one circuit when the other loses pressure, explains front/rear circuit pressure-loss behavior, calls bench bleeding important, and warns not to assume that a firm pedal alone proves all air has been removed.

## Diagnostic structure worth keeping

1. **Observe flow.** During a controlled two-person bleed, compare the actual discharge at the bleeders instead of judging only by pedal feel.
2. **Localize the fault.** If a hydraulic complaint remains, isolate sections of the system rather than replacing parts from the symptom alone.
3. **Master-cylinder isolation.** The video explicitly includes plugging/isolation at the master-cylinder ports. The practical inference is to compare pedal behavior with downstream circuits removed from the test. Treat the exact interpretation as pending full transcript/service-procedure review rather than inventing details.
4. **Separate pedal symptoms.** Excessive travel, a spongy feel, and a pedal that cannot hold pressure are related hydraulic complaints but are not synonyms.
5. **Do not use pedal feel as the only bleeding oracle.** A restriction can make the pedal feel firm while air remains elsewhere in the circuit.

## Important boundary

The Ram Man material strongly favors visible fluid-flow checks and component isolation. That is useful diagnostic discipline. However, the broad claim that normal-looking bleeder flow proves *all* components are correct is stronger than the evidence supports universally; a component can still have a mechanical, assist, modulation, or intermittent fault while fluid reaches a bleeder. Preserve the observation, but do not turn it into an absolute ASE rule.
