# Brakes

This is the brake side of the study material.

## Diagnostic frame

Separate four things that are often mixed together:

1. **pedal travel** — how far the pedal moves;
2. **pedal effort** — how hard it is to push;
3. **brake force** — how much a wheel actually slows the vehicle;
4. **release** — whether that brake lets go when pressure is removed.

A booster primarily changes effort. Hydraulic/mechanical faults can change travel, force, and release.

## Pedal feel

### Hard pedal

Think inadequate assist before assuming air in the hydraulic system. Depending on the vehicle: vacuum supply/check valve/booster, hydraulic boost, electric assist, or an assist-control problem.

### Long or low pedal

Possible families include:

- air or vapor in hydraulic fluid;
- excessive shoe/pad-to-rotor clearance;
- rear drum adjustment problem;
- pad knockback from rotor/hub/bearing movement or runout;
- hydraulic leak;
- master-cylinder problem;
- excessive mechanical travel.

The first question is whether **pumping changes it**.

### Pedal pumps up firmer/higher

That points toward travel/clearance/compressibility more than a simple lack of boost. Air/vapor, excessive clearance, poor drum adjustment, or pad knockback are examples.

### Pedal slowly sinks under steady force

Hydraulic pressure is not being held. Internal master-cylinder bypass is a classic cause, but external leakage and other hydraulic faults must be ruled out.

### Spongy pedal

Compressible gas in a system intended to be essentially incompressible is the classic mechanism. Air, overheated fluid producing vapor, or sometimes hose expansion can create the feel.

## Booster test is not a hydraulic test

For a conventional vacuum booster:

1. engine off;
2. pump pedal several times to exhaust stored vacuum;
3. hold steady pedal force;
4. start engine;
5. normal assist should make the pedal move somewhat as assist appears.

That asks whether assist arrives. It does **not** by itself explain a long pedal that pumps up, a dragging wheel, or a hydraulic leak.

## Brake pull

The vehicle generally yaws toward the side producing **more** braking force. A pull can therefore come from a grabbing brake on the pull side or a weak brake on the opposite side.

"Seized caliper" is not a complete diagnosis. A caliper can fail in different ways:

- piston/slides bind so the brake cannot apply normally → that side may be weak;
- piston/slides bind applied → that side drags and overheats;
- hose/restriction limits pressure going in → weak application;
- hose/restriction traps pressure on release → drag.

## Brake drag / one hot wheel

A wheel much hotter than its mate is strong evidence that energy is being dissipated at that corner. Possible causes include:

- caliper piston binding;
- caliper slide/pad binding;
- flexible hose acting as a one-way restriction;
- parking-brake mechanism not releasing;
- drum hardware/shoe problem;
- hydraulic pressure being held upstream;
- another wheel-end friction fault.

### Bleeder-release discriminator

With the vehicle safely supported and the brake confirmed dragging, opening that wheel's bleeder can distinguish **trapped hydraulic pressure** from **mechanical binding**:

- brake releases when pressure is vented → pressure was trapped upstream of the bleeder;
- brake remains stuck → investigate wheel-end mechanical binding.

## Heat can create a second symptom

A dragging brake can overheat the rotor/drum, friction material, caliper, and fluid. If brake fluid near the hot corner forms vapor, pedal travel can become long/spongy because vapor compresses.

One coherent chain is:

**drag → heat → fluid vapor/compressibility → longer pedal**.

It remains a hypothesis until distinguished from air, leakage, knockback, master-cylinder faults, or multiple simultaneous faults.

## Disc-pad wear patterns

Pad wear is evidence about relative movement and force:

- one pad much thinner than its mate can point toward piston/slide/caliper movement problems;
- tapered wear suggests the pad/caliper is not remaining square and free through its travel;
- both pads worn evenly but rapidly suggests a different problem from one pad hanging up.

Exact inboard/outboard interpretation depends on caliper design.

## Rotor geometry

Keep these distinct:

- **lateral runout**: rotor face wobbles side-to-side as it turns;
- **thickness variation**: rotor thickness changes around its circumference;
- **surface/friction variation**: friction conditions vary even when geometry is different.

Runout can contribute to pad knockback and can generate thickness variation over time. Thickness variation can create periodic pedal/brake-force pulsation.

## ABS / traction control / stability control

Core diagnostic chain:

**wheel motion → wheel-speed sensor/tone target → wiring/module input → control decision → hydraulic modulator/pump/valves → wheel brake pressure**.

When a warning light or unwanted activation occurs, determine whether the module saw bad information, made a bad decision, or could not execute the requested hydraulic action.

## Question habit

For Technician A / Technician B questions, evaluate each statement independently as true or false.

For every diagnosis question, rewrite it mentally as:

**symptom + conditions → candidate causes → one test that separates them**.
