# Case 001 — long pedal + pump-up + one very hot wheel

This case is preserved because it exposes several brake-diagnosis distinctions at once.

## Observations

The reported sequence was approximately:

- pedal initially felt somewhat long;
- very slow driving produced suspected grinding/dragging from the left-front area;
- later the pedal became substantially longer;
- pumping the pedal a second time made it firmer/higher;
- one brake/wheel became extremely hot.

Do not turn the suspected corner into a fact until temperature/inspection confirms which wheel was hot and what was dragging.

## First decomposition

There are at least two symptom classes:

1. **one wheel is generating abnormal heat** → a brake may not be releasing, or another wheel-end friction fault exists;
2. **pedal travel increases and pumps up** → the hydraulic/mechanical system contains excess travel or compressibility.

One fault can produce both symptoms, but two faults are also possible.

## High-priority hypothesis

A dragging brake overheats the wheel-end and brake fluid. Severe local heat can create vapor in the brake fluid. Vapor is compressible, so pedal travel can become long/spongy and can temporarily improve when pumped.

This is mechanically coherent with the observation set:

**drag → heat → fluid/vapor problem → longer pedal**.

It is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

## Other live hypotheses

- air already present in the hydraulic system;
- pad knockback / excessive pad-to-rotor clearance;
- wheel-bearing or hub movement causing knockback;
- rear drum clearance/adjustment problem if equipped;
- external hydraulic leak;
- master-cylinder internal bypass;
- caliper slide/piston binding without vapor formation;
- flexible hose trapping pressure at one wheel;
- parking-brake/mechanical binding where applicable;
- more than one simultaneous fault.

## Diagnostic order

Do not road-test a vehicle with a known severe brake overheat or unreliable pedal.

### 1. Cold visual inspection

At the overheated corner inspect:

- pad thickness and inner-vs-outer/taper pattern;
- rotor discoloration, cracking, scoring, or obvious heat damage;
- caliper boots/piston condition;
- slide freedom and pad fit in bracket;
- flexible hose damage/twist;
- leakage;
- hub/bearing play and roughness.

Compare to the opposite side.

### 2. Does that wheel rotate freely when brakes are released?

If no, establish whether the holding force is hydraulic or mechanical.

### 3. Bleeder-release discriminator

With safe support and correct brake-service procedure, open the dragging corner's bleeder.

- If the brake immediately releases, trapped hydraulic pressure is strongly implicated. Work upstream: flexible hose first as a local restriction candidate, then valves/master/booster pushrod/compensation path as architecture requires.
- If it remains stuck, the fault is mechanical at the wheel end: piston/slides/pads, parking-brake/drum hardware, or another rotating component.

### 4. Static pedal-hold test

After restoring the system to a safe condition and verifying fluid level/leaks, apply steady pedal force.

- pedal that holds position: less supportive of ongoing master-cylinder bypass;
- pedal that continues to sink: investigate internal bypass and external leakage.

### 5. Pump-up observation

Record whether the first press is long and the next one quickly becomes higher/firmer.

That favors excess clearance, knockback, air/vapor, or another travel/compressibility problem over a pure booster problem.

### 6. Separate booster check

On a conventional vacuum booster, exhaust stored vacuum with engine off, hold pedal force, then start the engine. A normal assist system should change pedal effort/position as vacuum assist appears.

This tests assist. It does not substitute for the hydraulic tests above.

### 7. Fluid condition and bleed

If a corner has been severely overheated, inspect the system according to service information and consider heat-damaged/contaminated fluid as evidence. Any opening of the hydraulic system requires correct bleeding and leak verification.

## Why the symptom sequence matters

The phrase "long pedal" alone is weak evidence. The combination is stronger:

- **longer after heat develops**;
- **second pump improves pedal**;
- **one corner is abnormally hot**.

The temporal structure is part of the diagnosis.
